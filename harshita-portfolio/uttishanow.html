UttishaNow

Designing movement as part of daily routines.

A behavioral design concept that helps young adults stay active through small, timely actions woven into everyday life, without relying on motivation or willpower.

ROLE Lead Researcher · Journey Mapper · Interaction Designer

Overview

Young adults know they should move more. They download apps, delete them in two weeks. UttishaNow asks a different question: what if the environment itself is broken?

Adults now spend 7.7–8.3 hours per day sedentary. This isn't a willpower story; it's an environment story. Work moved to desks. Commutes shortened. Leisure went digital. The environment defaults to stillness.

The Problem

The fitness app market has generated billions targeting sedentary adults. Most apps are used for a week, then deleted. The question was never why people stopped; it was what mechanism made them fail from the start.

Sedentary behavior above 7 hrs/day is an independent predictor of poor sleep quality. Move less → sleep worse → less energy to move. The cycle is self-reinforcing.

"Design the default. Movement will follow."

RESEARCH

01: What I Wanted to Understand

Secondary Research

Before recruiting a single participant, I ran a literature review focused on the sleep–sedentary–technology intersection. Three papers shaped the direction:

  • Movement, sleep, and mental state are entangled, not separate levers (Aschbrenner et al., 2022)
  • App abandonment isn't a UX problem; it's an expectation-reality gap (Attie & Meyer-Waarden, 2023)
  • Sedentary → poor sleep → low energy → sedentary: a self-reinforcing cycle (Sanchez-Trigo et al., 2024)
Environmental cues are a more reliable behavior driver than internal motivation. That finding became the hypothesis.

User Interviews

IRB-approved formal study. Six participants, semi-structured interviews (60–75 min) + 2–3 day real-time diary studies. Supervised by Prof. Christina Hanschke at DePaul.

Diary studies alongside interviews for one reason: people are unreliable narrators of their own behavior. A 48-hour diary gives you what actually happened.

INSIGHTS

02: Four Insights That Reshaped the Brief

01

Routine collapse

Sedentary behavior wasn't a choice; it was a collapse. 5 of 6 participants said their activity habits vanished the moment a structural scaffold (gym buddy, commute, team sport) disappeared.

So: don't target motivation. Target the scaffold.

02

Reminder fatigue

Notification-based reminders were universally ignored or disabled. Participants described them as "background noise." Frequency made it worse, not better.

So: no more reminders. Only interruptions that feel natural at the right moment.

03

Guilt spiral

Skipping one workout didn't just break a streak; it created guilt that made the next session less likely. Streak mechanics actively discouraged re-engagement after a lapse.

So: design for recovery, not perfection. Grace periods over binary streaks.

04

Micro windows exist

All 6 participants had at least 2–3 untapped micro-windows per day (waiting for coffee, ad breaks, bathroom trips) where movement was physically possible but never prompted.

So: target micro-windows, not full workout slots.

02 · Two archetypes, one deficit

Not personas. Behavioral patterns,
defined by what people lost.

ARCHETYPE 01

The Low-Energy Loafer

"I'm too tired to move."

Cognitive load depletes willpower; movement requires a decision that rarely happens on demanding days. The phone is the default coping mechanism.

ARCHETYPE 02

The Multitasking Juggler

"I'll do it when I have time."

That time never comes. Wants to be active, but a packed schedule leaves no clear window. Social context, not internal goal, is the primary driver.

Both share one thing: neither lacks motivation. Both lost an environmental enabler. The deficit is structural.

FRAMING

03: Problem Framing

Sedentary behavior is not a motivation failure; it is an environment failure. The problem isn't that people don't want to move. It's that the environment makes stillness the path of least resistance at every decision point.
HOW MIGHT WE

Make movement easier to start when the day is already busy, without adding guilt, pressure, or complexity?

PRINCIPLES

04: Design Principles

01 · Low-friction

Every action must require zero decisions

If it needs a conscious choice, it fails on the hardest days. Design for the lowest-energy state the user will ever be in.

02 · Context-aware

Match the suggestion to the user's real schedule

Generic suggestions fail. The app must know if you have 3 minutes or 30 before recommending anything.

03 · No shame

Design for recovery, not perfection

Skipping a day should make the next day easier to start, not harder. Grace periods over binary streaks.

04 · Small → enough

Micro-movement counts. Celebrate it.

A 2-minute walk during an ad break is a win. The system must treat it as one, consistently.

IDEATION

05: Three Directions Explored

CHOSEN ✓

Habit Cue Systems

Intervene at the two high-leverage windows: morning (first 10–15 min) and evening transition, using environmental and digital cues. Works without willpower.

DEPRIORITIZED

Gamification

Points, badges, leaderboards. Research showed motivation wasn't the barrier, and gamification compounds guilt when streaks break.

DEPRIORITIZED

Social Accountability

Buddy systems work (Vishu's story confirmed it) but depend on another person's availability and don't address solo movement needs.

WIREFRAMES

07: Lo-Fi Wireframes  ·  Less Dashboard, More Action

Early wireframes stripped the dashboard entirely. The first screen the user sees in the morning is a single action: not data, not stats, not history. Just: here's what you can do right now.

User Journey Map

Stage 1: Morning Routine
(Waking up & Preparing for the day)

During the 'Wake Up' stage of the journey, we observed:

  • Wake up and scroll through phone notifications.
  • Morning pace varies: relaxed or rushed routine.
  • Minimal movement; mostly in bed rather than moving.
  • Breakfast is optional, often skipped or on-the-go.
  • Energy levels are low; alertness builds gradually.
User Journey Map

Stage 2: Commute to Study Location

During the 'Commute' stage of the journey, we observed:

  • First significant movement of the day begins.
  • Commute methods vary: walk, bus, or train, all viable.
  • Commute method chosen depends on time, mood, and weather.
  • Many say walking offers fresh air and mental clarity.
  • Energy levels and convenience shape commute decisions.
User Journey Map

Stage 3: Study Session
with a Lunch Break

During the 'Focused Work/Study' stage of the journey, we observed:

  • Long periods of sitting at desks begin.
  • Users stay immersed in screens and tasks.
  • Breaks are often short and infrequent.
  • Common movement breaks are for coffee or a quick stroll around campus.
  • Physical movement remains minimal throughout the day.
User Journey Map

Stage 4: Evening Transition
(Returning Home and Leisure)

During the 'Evening Transition' stage of the journey, we observed:

  • Mood, weather, and fatigue influence transport decisions.
  • Users freshen up and shift to relaxation mode.
  • Evening activities: shows, scrolling, or socialising.
  • Relaxation is mostly sedentary; couch or bed are commonly preferred.

Wireframe Set 5

Wireframe Set 6

Final round of low-fidelity iterations before moving to hi-fi.

Two high-leverage windows emerged: Morning (Stages 1–2), the 10–15 minute window before passive defaults establish themselves; and Evening Transition (Stage 4), where the home environment is physically designed for stillness.
TESTING

What Broke in Testing

01

Home screen felt like a dashboard: data-heavy, not action-first.

Fix: Single "Start a break" action on home, one tap.

02

Reminder timing felt arbitrary. Users couldn't predict when alerts would come.

Fix: Daily and schedule-linked reminders synced to calendar when events actually end.

03

Streak counter created anxiety. Users said losing a streak made them want to quit entirely.

Fix: Rhythm view only. No streaks, no penalties. Weekly rhythm shown as a gentle pattern.

REFLECTION

Learnings & Next Steps

Learnings

  • Environment over motivation. Structural change outlasts any nudge. Design the path of least resistance first; motivation follows.
  • Constraints are the brief. Working within tight constraints (no willpower, no rebuild, no social dependency) produced more durable solutions than open-ended ideation.
  • Personalization is non-negotiable. Generic nudges failed universally. Schedule-aware suggestions had meaningfully higher completion.
  • Small friction, big change. Moving a remote 3 meters changed behavior more reliably than a push notification.

Next Steps

  • Run a dedicated usability session on sound cue effectiveness in the evening environment prototype.
  • Co-design session with participants: return diary data and ask them to locate friction points themselves.
  • Pressure-test archetypes across older age ranges, family structures, and shift workers.
  • Build a working calendar integration prototype to validate schedule-aware suggestion timing.
